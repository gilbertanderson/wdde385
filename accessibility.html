Accessibility & Inclusive Design

In plain language

Web accessibility means building sites that people with disabilities can use. That includes people who are blind or have low vision, people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people who cannot use a mouse, and people with cognitive or learning disabilities.

Inclusive design is the wider habit behind it: design for the widest range of people from the start, rather than fixing exclusions later. Most of the work is not exotic. It is using the right HTML element, making sure everything works from the keyboard, choosing readable colours, and writing clearly.

Why it matters

Roughly one person in six lives with a significant disability. If your site excludes them, it excludes a share of your audience larger than most browsers you bother to test.

It is also a legal requirement in a growing number of places. Public bodies and educational institutions in the United States are expected to meet Section 508, which is aligned with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, usually written WCAG. Similar obligations exist across the European Union and elsewhere. But compliance is a floor, not a goal. A site can pass every automated check and still be miserable to use.

The strongest argument is the curb-cut effect. Accommodations built for a permanent disability serve far more people than they were designed for. Captions serve a deaf viewer, a commuter in a noisy carriage, and a learner reading in a second language. Large touch targets serve someone with a tremor and anyone holding a phone one-handed. A layout that reflows at 320 pixels serves a low-vision user at 400 per cent zoom and every phone visitor you have.

Core guidance

WCAG organises its criteria under four principles, remembered as POUR: content must be Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust. The guidance below is the short version of what that means in practice.

Semantic HTML first

The single highest-value decision in accessibility is to use the HTML element that already means what you want. A <button> is focusable, operable with both Enter and Space, and announced as a button, with no extra work. A <div> styled to look like a button is none of those things until a large amount of ARIA and JavaScript rebuilds what the browser gave you for free, and that rebuild is where bugs live.

Landmark elements let screen reader users jump straight to a region instead of reading from the top. Headings do the same job inside the page, which is why the hierarchy must never skip a level: an <h4> directly after an <h2> tells a screen reader user that a section is missing.

Keyboard operation

Everything that works with a mouse must work with a keyboard. This is the baseline for people with motor disabilities, for screen reader users, and for anyone whose pointing device has failed. Test it by putting the mouse down and pressing Tab through the whole page.

  • Every interactive element must be reachable and operable.
  • Focus order must follow the visual order.
  • The focus indicator must be clearly visible and never hidden behind a sticky header or overlay.
  • There must be no keyboard trap: if Tab gets you in, Tab or Escape must get you out.
  • Content that scrolls, such as a wide code block, needs tabindex="0" so a keyboard user can scroll it.

Never remove the focus outline without replacing it with something better. outline: none with no substitute is the most common accessibility defect on the web.

Colour and contrast

Body text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background. Large text (24 pixels, or 19 pixels bold) needs 3 to 1. User interface components and the parts of graphics you must see to understand them also need 3 to 1. Check ratios with a contrast checker while you are choosing the palette, not after the site is built.

Colour must never be the only signal. A form field marked as wrong by turning red says nothing to a user with a colour vision deficiency, or to a screen reader user. Pair the colour with a symbol, a border change, and text.

Respect the settings people already have. Honour prefers-color-scheme, prefers-contrast, and prefers-reduced-motion, so someone who has configured their operating system gets the right experience with no per-site setup.

Text alternatives

Every image needs an alt attribute. What goes in it depends on the job the image does. An informative image gets a short description of the information it carries. A decorative image gets alt="", which tells a screen reader to skip it; leaving the attribute off entirely makes some screen readers read the file name instead. An image used as a link describes the destination, not the picture. A complex diagram gets a short alt plus a full description in the surrounding text, because one line cannot carry an architecture diagram.

Forms and errors

Forms are where accessibility most often fails, because errors are the hard part. Every input needs a real <label> tied to it by for and id. Placeholder text is not a label: it disappears when typing starts and usually fails contrast. Related controls belong in a <fieldset> with a <legend>. Format hints belong in aria-describedby so they are announced before the visitor types, not only after they fail.

When validation fails, mark each bad field with aria-invalid="true", put a summary of every problem at the top of the form, move focus to that summary, and link each item to its field. The contact form on this site does exactly that: submit it empty to see the pattern work.

Going further

Automated tools find about a third of the problems. axe DevTools, WAVE, and the Lighthouse accessibility audit reliably catch missing alternative text, low contrast, missing labels, and invalid ARIA. They cannot judge whether alternative text is meaningful or whether a heading structure is logical. A clean scan is a starting point, not a certificate.

Manual testing finds the rest. Keyboard-only testing is the highest-yield manual test per hour. Beyond that, test with real screen readers, and test more than one: NVDA with Firefox, JAWS with Chrome, VoiceOver with Safari, and TalkBack on Android differ meaningfully in their ARIA support. Add 400 per cent zoom, Windows High Contrast Mode, and a colour-blindness simulator.

The first rule of ARIA is not to use ARIA. Accessible Rich Internet Applications markup describes semantics the browser cannot infer. It changes nothing about behaviour: role="button" on a <div> does not make it focusable or make Enter work. Incorrect ARIA is worse than none, because it actively lies to assistive technology. When a custom widget is genuinely necessary, follow the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide for the expected keyboard behaviour.

Reference material. WCAG 2.2 at the World Wide Web Consortium is the normative standard; the WebAIM site at Utah State University is the friendliest practical explanation of it.
