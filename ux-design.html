User Experience Design

In plain language

User experience design, usually shortened to UX, is the work of making a website easy and pleasant to use. It asks a simple question about every screen: can the person in front of it do what they came to do, without confusion and without help?

UX is not decoration. Colour and typography matter, but they sit on top of decisions about what the site contains, how it is organised, what each page asks the visitor to do, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Why it matters

Bad user experience is expensive and mostly invisible. A visitor who cannot find the answer does not file a bug report. They leave, and the only trace is a number in an analytics dashboard that says nothing about why.

The cost is not shared evenly. A confusing checkout is a small annoyance for a practised shopper and a hard stop for someone using the site in a second language, on an old phone, on a train, with a screen reader, or under time pressure. Good UX widens the group of people who can complete the task; poor UX quietly narrows it.

UX work is also cheapest early. Changing a wireframe costs minutes, changing a built page costs hours, and changing a shipped product costs a release cycle plus the confusion of every user who learned the old version.

Core guidance

Usability heuristics

Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics remain the most useful short checklist in the field. Four of them carry most of the weight on a typical website:

  • Visibility of system status. The interface always tells the user what is happening. A saved form says it saved. A slow operation shows progress. Silence is interpreted as failure.
  • Match between the system and the real world. Use the words your readers use. "Website Optimization" is more findable than "Performance Engineering" because it is the phrase people actually search for.
  • Recognition rather than recall. Show the options instead of expecting people to remember them. Breadcrumbs, a visible current-page marker, and link text that describes its destination all remove memory work.
  • Help users recognise, diagnose, and recover from errors. An error message names the field, states the problem in plain words, and says how to fix it. "Invalid input" fails all three tests.

The heuristics are a review tool, not a design method. Walk an interface against them and you will find real problems fast, but they will not tell you what to build.

User research methods

Research methods differ along three dimensions: whether they capture what people say or what people do, whether they produce numbers or explanations, and the context in which they are run. No single method covers all three, which is why programmes that rely only on analytics keep being surprised.

  • Interviews and contextual inquiry explain motivation and reveal the workarounds people have already invented. Small samples; deep insight.
  • Usability testing (five participants attempting real tasks while thinking aloud) is the highest-yield method per hour invested. It shows what people do, not what they believe they do.
  • Surveys scale to thousands but record opinions, which routinely diverge from behaviour.
  • Analytics show where people struggle at scale (high exit pages, abandoned forms, searches that return nothing) but never why. Analytics generate questions; they do not answer them.
  • Card sorting and tree testing validate information architecture before any visual design exists.

Recruit participants who match the real audience, including people who use assistive technology and people reading in a second language. A convenience sample of colleagues tests whether the design works for people who already know the design.

UX artifacts

Artifacts exist to make thinking visible and arguable before code is written. Personas summarise research into a small number of goal-driven profiles. Journey maps lay out the whole path to a goal, including the steps that happen off the website, and expose the gaps between them. Wireframes settle layout and hierarchy while they are still cheap to change, deliberately in grey boxes so that reviewers argue about structure instead of colour. Prototypes make a flow clickable so it can be tested with people before it is built.

An artifact nobody reads is waste. Produce the smallest one that answers the question currently in dispute.

Interface writing

Most of a user interface is text, so most UX problems are writing problems. Front-load the point, keep sentences to roughly fifteen to twenty words, use the active voice, and prefer the common word over the formal one. Avoid idioms entirely: phrases like "out of the box" or "down to the wire" are exactly what fails readers whose first language is not English, and they defeat translation tools.

Link text is the highest-value writing on a page. Screen reader users often pull up a list of every link on the page and read it out of context, so "click here" appears as a list of identical, meaningless entries.

Going further

Three ideas repay study once the basics are in place.

Progressive disclosure. Show the common case first and let the reader open the detail. Every page on this site is built that way: a plain-language summary, then motivation, then implementation. It serves a first-semester student and a working developer from the same page without forcing either to read the other's version.

Forgiveness. Assume people will make mistakes and design the recovery, not just the happy path. Preserve what the user typed when validation fails. Never make a form time out. Never leave a page as a dead end.

Consistency. People build a model of a site in the first two or three pages. Moving the navigation, renaming a section, or changing what a button does destroys that model, and the damage falls hardest on people with cognitive disabilities and on screen reader users who navigate by landmark.

Two references worth keeping open: Nielsen's ten heuristics from the Nielsen Norman Group, and the plain-language guidance published at plainlanguage.gov.
